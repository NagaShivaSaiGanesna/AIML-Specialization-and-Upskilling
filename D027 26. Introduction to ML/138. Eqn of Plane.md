# Equation of a Straight Line, 3D Planes, and Hyperplanes

In machine learning algorithms like **Logistic Regression** and **Support Vector Machines (SVM)**, understanding the geometry of data is essential. Whether we are dealing with two dimensions or $n$-dimensions, the underlying mathematical principles remain consistent.

---

## 1. The Equation of a Straight Line (2D)

In a two-dimensional space with $x$ and $y$ axes, a straight line can be represented in several ways. You may be familiar with the slope-intercept form from basic algebra:

$$y = mx + c$$

In technical contexts and machine learning, we often use notations that are easier to scale to higher dimensions:

* **Standard Form:** $ax + by + c = 0$
* **Weight Notation:** $w_{1}x_{1} + w_{2}x_{2} + b = 0$

### Core Components

* **Slope ($m$):** This represents the "steepness." It denotes the change in the $y$-axis for every unit movement along the $x$-axis.
* **Intercept ($c$ or $b$):** This is the value of $y$ when $x = 0$. Geometrically, it is the point where the line crosses the $y$-axis.

### Vector Representation

We can simplify the equation $w_{1}x_{1} + w_{2}x_{2} + b = 0$ using vectors. If we let $w$ be a vector of coefficients $(w_{1}, w_{2})$ and $x$ be the input vector $(x_{1}, x_{2})$, the equation becomes:

$$w^{T}x + b = 0$$

If the line passes through the **origin** ($0,0$), the intercept $b$ becomes $0$, simplifying the equation to:


$$w^{T}x = 0$$

---

## 2. Moving to 3D Planes and Hyperplanes

As we add more dimensions (features), a "line" evolves into a "plane" and eventually a "hyperplane."

### 3D Plane

In a 3D space with axes $x_{1}, x_{2},$ and $x_{3}$, the equation expands to:


$$w_{1}x_{1} + w_{2}x_{2} + w_{3}x_{3} + b = 0$$

### Hyperplane ($n$-Dimensions)

In machine learning, we often work with hundreds of features. A **Hyperplane** is the generalization of a plane into $n$ dimensions. The formula remains structurally the same:


$$w_{1}x_{1} + w_{2}x_{2} + ... + w_{n}x_{n} + b = 0$$


Which is compactly written as:


$$w^{T}x + b = 0$$

---

## 3. Geometric Interpretation of $w$

A crucial concept in linear algebra is the relationship between the weight vector $w$ and the plane it defines.

### Orthogonality (Perpendicularity)

The vector $w$ is always **perpendicular** (normal) to the plane (or line). We can prove this using the dot product:

1. We know that $w^{T}x = 0$.
2. The dot product can also be written as: $||w|| \cdot ||x|| \cdot \cos(\theta) = 0$.
3. For this to be zero (assuming $w$ and $x$ are not zero vectors), $\cos(\theta)$ must be $0$.
4. This occurs when $\theta = 90^{\circ}$.

> **Key Takeaway:** The weight vector $w$ defines the orientation of the hyperplane by pointing directly away from it at a right angle.

---

## Comparison of Dimensions

| Dimension | Geometric Shape | Equation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **2D** | Straight Line | $w_{1}x_{1} + w_{2}x_{2} + b = 0$ |
| **3D** | Plane | $w_{1}x_{1} + w_{2}x_{2} + w_{3}x_{3} + b = 0$ |
| **nD** | Hyperplane | $w^{T}x + b = 0$ |

---

## 1. The Role of the Bias (Intercept)

When a plane does **not** pass through the origin, we include the bias term $b$ (also called the intercept). The equation for every point $x$ on that plane is:

$$w^{T}x + b = 0$$

If you try to calculate the dot product $w^{T}x$ for a point on this plane, it won't be $0$; it will be exactly $-b$.

---

## 2. The "Origin" Trick (Vector Augmentation)

In machine learning, we hate carrying around that $+ b$ everywhere. It makes the calculus messy. To "force" the plane to look like it passes through the origin in a higher dimension, we use a trick called **Augmentation**.

We add a "dummy" feature to our data and a "dummy" weight to our vector:

1. **Input Vector ($x$):** We add a constant $1$ to every data point.
2. **Weight Vector ($w$):** We include $b$ as $w_{0}$.

Now, the dot product looks like this:

$$w_{new} = [b, w_{1}, w_{2}, \dots, w_{n}]$$

$$x_{new} = [1, x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{n}]$$

When you perform the dot product now:


$$w_{new}^{T}x_{new} = (b \cdot 1) + (w_{1} \cdot x_{1}) + \dots + (w_{n} \cdot x_{n}) = 0$$

By adding that extra dimension, we have mathematically "rotated" the problem so that the hyperplane **always passes through the origin** of this new, higher-dimensional space.

---

## 3. Why $w$ is Still Perpendicular

Even if the plane is shifted away from the origin by $b$, the **direction** of the plane doesn't change—only its **position**.

Think of it this way:

* If you have a flat sheet of paper on a table, the vector pointing straight up is $w$.
* If you lift that paper 5 inches off the table (changing $b$), the vector pointing straight up is **still** $w$.

To prove $w$ is perpendicular to a shifted plane, take any two points $x_{1}$ and $x_{2}$ that both lie on the plane:

1. $w^{T}x_{1} + b = 0$
2. $w^{T}x_{2} + b = 0$

Subtract the two equations:


$$(w^{T}x_{1} + b) - (w^{T}x_{2} + b) = 0$$

$$w^{T}(x_{1} - x_{2}) = 0$$

The term $(x_{1} - x_{2})$ represents a vector that lies **perfectly flat** on the plane. Since the dot product of $w$ and any "flat" vector on the plane is $0$, $w$ must be perpendicular to the plane, regardless of where the plane sits in space.

---

### Summary Table: Origin vs. Shifted

| Feature | Passing through Origin | Not passing through Origin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Equation** | $w^{T}x = 0$ | $w^{T}x + b = 0$ |
| **Dot Product** | Equals $0$ | Equals $-b$ |
| **Orthogonality** | $w$ is perpendicular to $x$ | $w$ is perpendicular to the *plane* |
| **ML Representation** | $w^{T}x$ | $w^{T}x + b$ (or Augmented $w^{T}x$) |
