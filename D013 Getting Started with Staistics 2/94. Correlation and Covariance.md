# Covariance and Correlation

Understanding the relationship between continuous variables is essential for data analysis and machine learning. This guide explores how **Covariance** and **Correlation** allow us to quantify these relationships numerically.

---

## 1. Covariance

**Covariance** is a statistical measure that indicates the direction of the linear relationship between two random variables. It tells us whether variables tend to increase or decrease together.

### Core Concepts:

* **Positive Covariance:** As variable X increases, variable Y tends to increase as well (e.g., house size vs. house price).
* **Negative Covariance:** As variable X increases, variable Y tends to decrease (e.g., house being haunted vs. house price).
* **Zero Covariance:** No linear relationship exists between the variables.

### The Formula:

For a sample of size **n**, the covariance between X and Y is:

**Cov(x, y) = [Summation of (x_i - x-bar) * (y_i - y-bar)] / (n - 1)**

* **x-bar / y-bar:** Sample means of X and Y.
* **n - 1:** Used for sample data (Bessel's correction).

> **Pro Tip:** Covariance of a variable with itself, **Cov(x, x)**, is exactly equal to the **Variance** of x.

### The Limitation:

Covariance can range from **negative infinity to positive infinity**. Because it is not scaled, you cannot determine the **strength** of the relationship. A covariance of 100 isn't necessarily "stronger" than a covariance of 20 because the value depends on the units of the data.

---

## 2. Pearson Correlation Coefficient

To fix the scaling issue of covariance, we use **Pearson Correlation**. It normalizes the covariance by the standard deviations of the variables, restricting the output to a range between **-1 and +1**.

**Formula:**
**rho(x, y) = Cov(x, y) / (sigma_x * sigma_y)**

### Interpreting the Values:

| Value | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **+1** | Perfect Positive Linear Correlation |
| **0** | No Linear Correlation |
| **-1** | Perfect Negative Linear Correlation |

---

## 3. Spearman Rank Correlation

Pearson correlation is excellent for **linear** data (straight lines), but it fails to capture **non-linear** relationships (curves). **Spearman Rank Correlation** solves this by calculating correlation based on the **ranks** of the data points rather than the raw values.

### Why use Spearman?

1. **Non-linear Data:** It captures **monotonic** relationships where X and Y move in the same direction but not at a constant rate.
2. **Outliers:** Since it uses ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd...), it is much more robust to outliers than Pearson.

**Formula:**
**r_s = Cov(rank_x, rank_y) / (std_rank_x * std_rank_y)**

---

## 4. Real-World Use Case: Feature Selection

In Data Science and Machine Learning, correlation is used for **Feature Selection** to identify which variables are important for predicting a target (e.g., House Price).

### Example: Predicting House Price

| Feature | Correlation with Price | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Size of House** | Positive (~0.9) | **KEEP:** Highly relevant. |
| **No. of Rooms** | Positive (~0.8) | **KEEP:** Relevant feature. |
| **Haunted?** | Negative (-0.7) | **KEEP:** Important negative impact. |
| **No. of Occupants** | Near Zero (~0.01) | **DROP:** Irrelevant to price. |

By dropping features with near-zero correlation, you simplify your model and prevent it from learning "noise."
