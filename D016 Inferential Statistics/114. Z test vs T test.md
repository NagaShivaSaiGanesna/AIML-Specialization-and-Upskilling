# Choosing Between Z-Tests and T-Tests

In statistical hypothesis testing, selecting the correct distribution is critical for reaching an accurate conclusion. The choice primarily depends on two factors: your knowledge of the **Population Standard Deviation (sigma)** and the **Sample Size (n)**.

---

## The Decision Framework

To determine whether to use a **Z-Test** or a **T-Test**, you must follow a specific logical path. This ensures that the mathematical assumptions of your test match the data you have available.

### 1. Is the Population Standard Deviation (sigma) Known?

This is the most important starting point. In real-world scenarios, we rarely know the true parameters of an entire population, but in theoretical or well-documented industrial processes, **sigma** might be provided.

* **If NO:** You cannot use a Z-Test. You must use a **T-Test**. In this case, you will use the **Sample Standard Deviation (s)** as an estimate for the population.
* **If YES:** You move to the second criteria: the **Sample Size**.

### 2. Is the Sample Size (n) Greater Than 30?

The number **30** is a common threshold in statistics based on the **Central Limit Theorem**.

* **If YES (n > 30):** If you know **sigma** and your sample is large, use the **Z-Test**. Large samples tend to follow a **Normal Distribution** more closely.
* **If NO (n <= 30):** Even if you know **sigma**, a small sample size introduces more uncertainty. Therefore, you should use the **T-Test**.

---

## Comparison of Z-Test vs. T-Test

The following table summarizes the key differences and requirements for each test:

| Feature | Z-Test | T-Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Population Variance** | Must be **Known** (**sigma**) | Usually **Unknown** (uses **s**) |
| **Sample Size (n)** | Typically **n > 30** | Typically **n < 30** |
| **Distribution Shape** | Normal Distribution | T-Distribution (wider tails) |
| **Reliability** | High for large datasets | High for small datasets |

---

## Key Terminology

* **Population Standard Deviation (sigma):** A measure of how spread out the numbers are in the entire group you are studying.
* **Sample Standard Deviation (s):** An estimate of the spread calculated only from the small group (sample) you actually measured.
* **Sample Size (n):** The total number of observations or data points in your sample.
* **T-Distribution:** Similar to a normal distribution but with "heavier tails," meaning it accounts for a higher likelihood of extreme values when dealing with small samples.

---

## Summary Logic Flow

1. **Start:** Do I have **sigma**?
2. **No sigma:** Use **T-Test**.
3. **Have sigma:** Check **n**.
4. **n > 30:** Use **Z-Test**.
5. **n <= 30:** Use **T-Test**.

> **Pro Tip:** In modern data science, if you are ever in doubt or the sample size is borderline, the **T-Test** is generally considered the "safer" choice because the T-distribution converges to the Z-distribution as the sample size increases.
