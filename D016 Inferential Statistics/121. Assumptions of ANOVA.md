# Assumptions of ANOVA

Before performing an **Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)**, it is essential to ensure that your data meets specific criteria. If these assumptions are violated, the results of the test may be unreliable or misleading. Here is a detailed breakdown of the four core assumptions.

---

## 1. Normality of Sampling Distribution of Means

This assumption states that the distribution of the sample means must be **normally distributed** (following a Gaussian distribution).

* **Key Concept:** This is closely related to the **Central Limit Theorem**. It implies that even if the population isn't perfectly normal, the distribution of the means of your samples should be.
* **Visual Check:** You can verify this using a **Q-Q Plot** or a **Histogram**.

---

## 2. Absence of Outliers

ANOVA is highly sensitive to **outliers** (extreme values). Because ANOVA relies on the mean and the variance, a single extreme score can significantly skew the results.

* **Requirement:** Outlying scores need to be identified and removed or handled before running the test.
* **Detection Methods:** * **Box Plots:** To visually identify points outside the "whiskers."
* **Interquartile Range (IQR):** A mathematical method to define the boundaries for normal data.



---

## 3. Homogeneity of Variance (Homoscedasticity)

This assumption requires that the **population variance** among the different levels of your independent variable is equal.

* **Mathematical Notation:** sigma1 squared = sigma2 squared = sigma3 squared
* **Explanation:** The "spread" of the data in each group should be approximately the same. If one group has a very narrow spread and another is very wide, the ANOVA test results become less accurate.
* **Testing:** This is often checked using **Levene’s Test** or **Bartlett’s Test**.

---

## 4. Independent and Random Sampling

The final assumption concerns how the data was collected.

* **Independence:** The observations in one group should not be influenced by or related to the observations in another group. Each data point must stand on its own.
* **Randomness:** Samples should be selected randomly from the population to ensure the results are generalizable and free from selection bias.

---

## Summary of Assumptions

| Assumption | What it Means | Why it Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Normality** | Means follow a bell curve | Ensures the math behind the F-statistic is valid. |
| **No Outliers** | No extreme data points | Prevents the mean from being misleadingly pulled. |
| **Homogeneity** | Equal variance across groups | Ensures groups are comparable on the same scale. |
| **Independence** | Data points are unrelated | Prevents patterns caused by external hidden links. |

---

## Technical Note for Interviews

If an interviewer asks what to do if these assumptions are not met, you can mention **Non-Parametric** alternatives. For example, if the normality assumption is failed, the **Kruskal-Wallis Test** is often used as the non-parametric version of a One-Way ANOVA.
