# Understanding Polynomial Regression: Modeling Non-Linear Relationships

In our previous discussions, we focused on **Simple** and **Multiple Linear Regression**, where the relationship between input and output is assumed to be a straight line or a flat plane. However, real-world data is often more complex. This guide explores **Polynomial Regression**, a powerful technique for capturing non-linear trends.

---

## 1. Why Linear Models Fail on Curvilinear Data

In **Simple Linear Regression**, we aim to create a straight **best-fit line**. If we have two input features, we create a **3D Plane**, and for more than two, a **Hyperplane**.

The limitation arises when your data points follow a curve rather than a straight line.

* **Linear Fit:** If you force a straight line onto curved data, the **Residual (Error)** between the truth value and the prediction will be extremely high.
* **Polynomial Fit:** By using polynomial regression, we can "bend" the best-fit line to capture the underlying pattern, significantly reducing the loss value.

---

## 2. The Polynomial Equation and Degrees

The complexity of a polynomial model is determined by its **Degree**. Let’s look at how the hypothesis equation, $h_θ(x)$, evolves as we increase the degree for a single independent feature.

### Degree 0: The Constant

When the degree is 0, the equation is simply a constant.


$$h_θ(x) = \beta_0 \cdot x^0 = \beta_0$$


This represents a horizontal line that does not change with the input.

### Degree 1: Simple Linear Regression

A degree of 1 gives us the standard linear equation.


$$h_θ(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot x^1$$

### Degree 2: Quadratic Regression

At degree 2, we introduce a squared term, allowing the line to curve once (creating a parabola).


$$h_θ(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot x^1 + \beta_2 \cdot x^2$$

### Degree N: General Polynomial Form

For any degree $n$, the model expands to:


$$h_θ(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x^1 + \beta_2 x^2 + \dots + \beta_n x^n$$

---

## 3. Polynomial Degrees and Model Performance

Choosing the right degree is a critical balancing act.

| Degree Level | Performance State | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Too Low (e.g., Degree 1)** | **Underfitting** | The model is too simple to see the curve; error is high on both training and test data. |
| **Optimal Degree** | **Generalized** | The model captures the trend without following the noise; performs well on new data. |
| **Too High (e.g., Degree 15)** | **Overfitting** | The model becomes "wiggly" to touch every point. It has zero error on training data but fails miserably on new data. |

---

## 4. Multiple Polynomial Regression

When we have multiple independent features (e.g., $x_1$ and $x_2$), a degree of 2 doesn't just square the terms; it also accounts for interactions.

* **Degree 1:** $h_θ(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2$
* **Degree 2:** $h_θ(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_1^2 + \beta_4 x_2^2$

*(Note: In advanced implementations, interaction terms like $\beta_5 x_1 x_2$ are also often included to capture how features affect each other.)*

---

## 5. Summary of Key Concepts

* **Non-Linearity:** Use polynomial regression when the relationship between variables is not a straight line.
* **Catching the Trend:** The goal is to "capture" or identify the non-linear relationship to reduce prediction error.
* **Hyperparameter Tuning:** The "Degree" is a parameter you must choose carefully to avoid the overfitting trap.
