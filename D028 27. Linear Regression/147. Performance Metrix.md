# Linear Regression Performance Metrics: R-Squared and Adjusted R-Squared

To determine if a linear regression model is truly capturing the patterns in your data, we use specific performance metrics. While mean squared error tells us the magnitude of error, **R-Squared** and **Adjusted R-Squared** tell us how much of the data's variance is explained by the model.

---

## 1. R-Squared (Coefficient of Determination)

**R-Squared** measures the proportion of the variance for a dependent variable that is explained by the independent variables in a regression model.

### The Formula

$$R^{2} = 1 - \frac{SS_{residual}}{SS_{total}}$$

| Term | Mathematical Notation | Definition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sum of Squared Residuals** | Σ(yᵢ - ŷᵢ)² | The total error between the **true points** (yᵢ) and the **predicted points** (ŷᵢ) on the best fit line. |
| **Sum of Squared Total** | Σ(yᵢ - ȳ)² | The total variance in the data compared to the **mean** (ȳ) of all y-values. |

### Interpretation

* **Range:** Typically 0 to 1 (0% to 100%).
* **Value → 1:** The model explains nearly all the variance. It is highly accurate.
* **Value → 0:** The model explains none of the variance and is no better than simply predicting the average value for every point.

---

## 2. The Problem with R-Squared

A major limitation of R-Squared is that it **always increases** (or stays the same) as you add more independent variables, even if those variables are completely irrelevant to the prediction.

**Example:**

1. You predict house prices using **Size**. R-Squared = 0.75.
2. You add **Number of Bedrooms**. R-Squared increases to 0.80.
3. You add **The gender of the owner**. R-Squared might still increase to 0.82, even though gender has no logical correlation with price.

Because R-Squared cannot decrease, it can mislead you into thinking a complex model is better than a simple one, leading to **Overfitting**.

---

## 3. Adjusted R-Squared

**Adjusted R-Squared** is a modified version of R-Squared that has been adjusted for the number of predictors (features) in the model. It effectively "penalizes" you for adding features that do not improve the model's predictive power.

### The Formula

$$Adjusted R^{2} = 1 - \left[\frac{(1 - R^{2})(n - 1)}{n - p - 1}\right]$$

* **n:** Number of data points (sample size).
* **p:** Number of independent features (predictors).

### How it behaves:

| Scenario | Impact on Adjusted R-Squared |
| --- | --- |
| **Relevant Feature Added** | Increases (if the improvement in $R^{2}$ outweighs the penalty of $p$). |
| **Irrelevant Feature Added** | **Decreases** (the increase in $p$ outweighs any tiny, random gain in $R^{2}$). |

---

## 4. Comparison Summary

| Metric | Sensitivity to New Features | Best Use Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R-Squared** | Increases with every feature. | Simple linear regression with few variables. |
| **Adjusted R-Squared** | Only increases if the feature is significant. | **Multiple Linear Regression** to avoid feature bloat. |

> **Founder's Insight:** Always look at Adjusted R-Squared when building complex models. If your R-Squared is going up but your Adjusted R-Squared is going down, it’s a clear signal that you are adding "noise" to your model and should simplify your feature set.
