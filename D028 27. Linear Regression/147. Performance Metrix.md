# Linear Regression Performance Metrics: R-Squared and Adjusted R-Squared

When evaluating a model, Mean Squared Error (MSE) tells us the "distance" of our mistakes, but it doesn't tell us the **strength** of the relationship. **R-Squared** and **Adjusted R-Squared** provide a percentage-based score that describes how much of the "story" our model is actually telling.

---

## 1. R-Squared (Coefficient of Determination)

**R-Squared** ($R^2$) represents the percentage of the variance in the dependent variable ($y$) that is predictable from the independent variables ($x$).

### The Mathematical Intuition

$$R^{2} = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}$$

To understand this, we compare our model to a "Baseline Model." The baseline is the simplest possible prediction: the **mean** ($\bar{y}$) of all values.

| Term | Mathematical Notation | Intuition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sum of Squared Residuals ($SS_{res}$)** | $\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ | The "Unexplained Variance": Error left over after using our model. |
| **Sum of Squared Total ($SS_{tot}$)** | $\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2$ | The "Total Variance": Error if we just guessed the average for everything. |

### Interpretation

* **$R^2 = 1.0$ (100%):** The model fits the data perfectly. All points are on the line.
* **$R^2 = 0.0$ (0%):** The model is no better than just guessing the average.
* **$R^2 < 0$:** This actually happens if your model is worse than the mean (usually due to a non-intercept or highly biased model).

---

## 2. The Trap: R-Squared Inflation

A critical pitfall of $R^2$ is its **non-decreasing nature**. As you add features to a model—no matter how useless—the $R^2$ will mathematically increase or stay the same.

* **The Logic:** Even a random variable (like the "color of the buyer's shirt") might, by pure chance, correlate slightly with the target. $R^2$ sees this tiny random correlation and adds it to the score.
* **The Result:** This encourages **Overfitting**, making you believe your model is becoming more accurate when it is actually just becoming more cluttered with "noise."

---

## 3. Adjusted R-Squared: The "BS Detector"

**Adjusted R-Squared** is the professional's metric. It applies a mathematical penalty for every feature ($p$) added to the model.

### The Formula

$$Adjusted R^{2} = 1 - \left[\frac{(1 - R^{2})(n - 1)}{n - p - 1}\right]$$

* **$n$:** Total sample size (number of data points).
* **$p$:** Number of independent variables (predictors).

### Why the denominator ($n - p - 1$) matters:

As you increase the number of features ($p$), the denominator becomes smaller, which makes the subtracted portion larger. This causes the **Adjusted $R^2$** to drop unless the increase in $R^2$ is significant enough to offset the penalty.

| Feature Type | Effect on $R^2$ | Effect on Adjusted $R^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Relevant Feature** | Significant Increase | **Increases** |
| **Irrelevant/Noise Feature** | Slight Increase | **Decreases** |

---

## 4. Summary Comparison

| Metric | Behavior with Extra Features | When to Trust It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R-Squared** | Biased upward; always increases. | When performing **Simple Linear Regression** (1 variable). |
| **Adjusted R-Squared** | Unbiased; penalizes complexity. | When performing **Multiple Linear Regression** (2+ variables). |

---

## 5. FAANG-Level System Design & "What If" Questions

* **System Design (Model Selection):** You are building a real-time ad-bidding engine for YouTube. You have two versions of a model: **Model A** ($R^2$: 0.85, Adj $R^2$: 0.82) and **Model B** ($R^2$: 0.86, Adj $R^2$: 0.79). Which model do you deploy to production, and why?
* **The "What If" of Sample Size ($n$):** Look at the Adjusted $R^2$ formula. What happens to the gap between $R^2$ and Adjusted $R^2$ as your dataset grows to 100 million rows ($n \to \infty$)? Is the penalty for adding features still significant at that scale?
* **The "What If" of Near-Perfect Multicollinearity:** If you add a feature that is a 99% copy of an existing feature, how will it affect the $SS_{res}$? Will the Adjusted $R^2$ help you realize that the new feature is redundant?
* **The "What If" of Domain Specificity:** In social sciences (predicting human behavior), an $R^2$ of 0.3 is often considered a success. In physics, an $R^2$ of 0.9 is often considered a failure. Why does the "goodness" of an $R^2$ score depend on the field of study?



Here are the deep-dive answers to the performance metric challenges. These scenarios test whether you can interpret the math in a high-pressure production environment.

---

## 1. System Design: Model A vs. Model B

* **The Decision:** You deploy **Model A**.
* **The Reasoning:** Even though Model B has a slightly higher $R^2$ ($0.86$ vs $0.85$), its **Adjusted $R^2$ dropped significantly** ($0.79$ vs $0.82$).
* **FAANG Perspective:** This tells us that Model B is **overfitting**. It has added features that provide a tiny bit of random gain but increase the complexity (the $p$ value) so much that the model's reliability decreases. In production, a simpler model (Model A) is more robust, easier to maintain, and likely to generalize better to new YouTube users.

---

## 2. What If: The Impact of "Big Data" Scale ($n \to \infty$)

* **The Math:** Look at the term $\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}$. As $n$ (sample size) becomes massive (e.g., 100 million), that fraction approaches **1**.
* **The Result:** At massive scales, the penalty for adding features becomes negligible. If you have trillions of data points, you can afford to add hundreds of features without the Adjusted $R^2$ penalizing you.
* **The Pitfall:** This is dangerous! Even if Adjusted $R^2$ doesn't drop, adding 1,000 features to a 100-million-row dataset increases **computational latency** and **storage costs**. In FAANG, we often use **Regularization** (L1/L2) instead of just relying on Adjusted $R^2$ because we care about the "cost to serve" the model.

---

## 3. What If: Near-Perfect Multicollinearity

* **The Effect:** If a new feature is a 99% copy of an old one, the $SS_{res}$ (Sum of Squared Residuals) will barely move because the model already "knew" that information.
* **The Metric Signal:** Since $R^2$ won't increase much, but the number of predictors ($p$) *does* increase, the **Adjusted $R^2$ will decrease**.
* **The Insight:** This is exactly why we use Adjusted $R^2$. It flags redundant features that add complexity without adding "information gain."

---

## 4. What If: Domain Specificity (Physics vs. Social Science)

* **Social Sciences ($R^2 = 0.3$ is good):** Human behavior is chaotic and influenced by millions of unmeasurable variables (mood, upbringing, weather). If a model can explain 30% of why people click an ad, that is a goldmine for a company like Meta.
* **Physics ($R^2 = 0.9$ is bad):** Laws of physics (like gravity or electricity) are precise. If you are modeling the trajectory of a rocket and your $R^2$ is only 0.9, it means you are missing a massive fundamental force, and your rocket will likely crash.
* **The Takeaway:** $R^2$ is a **relative** metric. You must always compare it to the "state of the art" in your specific industry.

---

## Summary of Metric Sensitivity

| Metric | Goal | High-Scale Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MSE / RMSE** | Minimize error distance | Affected by units (e.g., dollars vs. cents) |
| **R-Squared** | Maximize variance explained | Always goes up; "Optimistic" |
| **Adjusted R-Squared** | Maximize information efficiency | Penalizes "noise"; "Realistic" |
