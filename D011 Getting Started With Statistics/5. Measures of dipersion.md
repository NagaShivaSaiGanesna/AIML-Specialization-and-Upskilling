# Measures of Dispersion: Variance

While **Measures of Central Tendency** tell us where the center of our data lies, **Measures of Dispersion** describe the spread or "scatter" of the data points around that center. Understanding dispersion is crucial because two datasets can have the exact same mean but look completely different in terms of consistency.

---

## 1. Defining Dispersion

Dispersion indicates how much the data varies from the average. If the data points are close to the mean, the dispersion is low (the data is consistent). If the points are far from the mean, the dispersion is high (the data is spread out).

### Comparison of Two Distributions

Imagine two groups of ages with the same mean age of **3**:

| Distribution | Data Points | Mean | Spread Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Group A** | 2, 2, 4, 4 | 3 | Points are very close to 3. |
| **Group B** | 1, 1, 5, 5 | 3 | Points are further away from 3. |

Even though the "typical" age is the same, Group B has higher dispersion. We measure this mathematically using **Variance**.

---

## 2. Population Variance

**Population Variance** (sigma squared) measures the average of the squared deviations from the mean for an entire population.

### The Formula

**sigma^2 = sum(x - mu)^2 / N**

* **sigma^2**: Population Variance symbol.
* **x**: Individual data points.
* **mu**: Population mean.
* **N**: Total population size.

### Why do we square the differences?

We square **(x - mu)** to ensure all values are positive. If we simply added the differences without squaring, the positive and negative distances from the mean would cancel each other out, resulting in a sum of zero.

---

## 3. Step-by-Step Calculation

Let's calculate the variance for our two groups to see how the numbers reflect the spread.

### Calculation for Group B (1, 1, 5, 5)

1. **Find the Mean (mu):** (1 + 1 + 5 + 5) / 4 = **3**.
2. **Subtract Mean and Square:**
* (1 - 3)^2 = (-2)^2 = **4**
* (1 - 3)^2 = (-2)^2 = **4**
* (5 - 3)^2 = (2)^2 = **4**
* (5 - 3)^2 = (2)^2 = **4**


3. **Sum of Squares:** 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = **16**.
4. **Divide by N:** 16 / 4 = **4**.

* **Variance (sigma^2) = 4**

In contrast, Group A (2, 2, 4, 4) results in a variance of **1**. The higher number for Group B confirms it is more "dispersed."

---

## 4. Sample Variance

When we are dealing with a **Sample** (a subset of the population), the formula changes slightly to provide a more accurate estimate of the true population variance.

### The Formula

**s^2 = sum(x - x-bar)^2 / (n - 1)**

* **s^2**: Sample Variance symbol.
* **x-bar**: Sample mean.
* **n**: Sample size.
* **n - 1**: This adjustment is known as **Bessel's Correction**.

### Why n - 1?

Using **n** in the denominator for samples tends to underestimate the actual population variance (it is a "biased" estimator). By dividing by **n - 1**, we mathematically correct this bias, making the result more accurate for predicting the population behavior.

---

## 5. Summary Table: Population vs. Sample

| Feature | Population Variance | Sample Variance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Symbol** | sigma^2 | s^2 |
| **Mean used** | mu | x-bar |
| **Denominator** | N | n - 1 |
